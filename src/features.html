Earth Jukebox

NASA's Earth data, made to be heard. Walk across a map with your arrow keys and listen to what is there.

Open the app
The idea

How it works

Most satellite maps are pictures, which leaves out anyone who cannot see them. Earth Jukebox turns real NASA satellite data into sound you can move through, using headphones and a keyboard.

  1. Pick a layer: rain, land heat, plants, ocean heat or haze.
  2. A soft pulse beats from Null Island (0, 0), where the equator meets the prime meridian, shown as a glowing orb on a map of the whole world. You start a little way off. Walk toward it with the arrow keys, or walk anywhere else on Earth.
  3. It gets louder as you get closer, and you hear which side it is on.
  4. An instrument joins in where the data is intense: violin for rain, cello for land heat, flute for plants, clarinet for ocean heat and French horn for haze. The stronger the data under you, the louder and brighter it plays. Press 6 for the Earth Orchestra: all five at once.
Data

Five map layers

Each layer is a different part of the planet, from a different NASA dataset. Each has its own instrument, so you can tell them apart by ear. Layers are served live by NASA GIBS.

Satellites cannot see through clouds for some layers, so there are gaps in heat, plants and haze. Gaps are silent.

Listening

What you hear

  • Spatial audioBinaural 3D sound (HRTF) from the browser's Web Audio engine. Use headphones and you can tell left from right, and ahead from behind.
  • Distance as volumeVolume falls off smoothly with distance and never drops to silence, so you can always follow the pulse back to Null Island, from anywhere on Earth.
  • The Earth OrchestraPress 6 and all five layers play at once, each on its own instrument. Together they spell one open A major chord, spread from the cello's low A up to the flute's high E, so any mix sounds calm and full: walk across the planet and hear its chord change, from a lone clarinet over the open ocean to violin, cello, flute and horn over a hot, green, stormy, hazy coast. The map shows every layer at once, each in its instrument's color: violin blue, cello red, flute green, clarinet teal, horn gold.
  • Two jobs, never mixedA soft pulse at Null Island (0, 0) tells you where the center is: one warm, round tone per beat that pans and fades with distance. The instruments tell you what is under you, louder and brighter as the data gets more intense. They do not pan or fade as you walk, so their loudness only ever means the data.
  • Always know your layerEvery switch is announced ("Layer 2 of 6: Land heat, played by the cello", or just "Earth Orchestra") and plays that instrument's note, like a sound logo. Press L any time to hear it again.
  • Intensity is loudness and brightnessThe layer's note gets louder and its tone opens up as the data intensifies, so strong areas sound sharper as well as louder.
  • A natural voice, free and open sourceAnnouncements are spoken by Kokoro-82M, an open-source text-to-speech model that runs in your own browser on the graphics card. There is no account or key, and nothing you hear is sent anywhere. It downloads once (about 160 MB) and is then cached. The announcements you hear most are prepared ahead of time and start instantly, new sentences start in about half a second, and the browser's own voice covers any gaps. It needs WebGPU (recent Chrome or Edge), so phones keep the browser voice for now.
  • Sound keyButtons that play each level on demand (for example light, moderate, heavy, extreme), so you can learn what the sounds mean before you explore.
Exploring

Moving and understanding

  • Walk the mapArrow keys or WASD, with Shift to move faster. On a phone or tablet, press anywhere on the map and drag like a joystick: pull a little to walk, farther to go faster. You always face north.
  • VibrationOn phones that support it, the device buzzes when you walk into stronger data, with a stronger pattern for stronger levels. It can be switched off.
  • Spoken summary on demandPress H for a short spoken line such as "Heavy rain. France, Europe." It names the country and continent, or the sea, using public-domain Natural Earth outlines that load with the page, so it works without any extra service. It is short on purpose, so you only hear it when you ask. The text is also kept in the page for screen readers.
  • Plain-word levelsLevels use words like light, heavy and scorching instead of color names, so they mean something whether or not you have ever seen color.
  • 3D globeThe page opens on a real 3D Earth built from NASA imagery: relief, city lights at night and the active data layer on top. A glowing dot shows where you are on the planet, and the globe turns to follow you as you walk. Drag to spin it; it settles back on Null Island. The glow around the planet swells as the data under you gets more intense.
  • 3D terrain viewThe 3D view is a landscape where intensity becomes height, so a storm is a mountain, and it follows you across the whole planet. The flat map shows the entire world at once, with NASA relief under the data. A glowing beam marks the sound source and the camera follows you from behind, facing north, the same direction the sound is oriented. Press V to switch to the flat map.
  • Live readoutThe panel under the map shows the level at your position, the direction to Null Island, your distance in kilometers, the volume, the date, and your real latitude and longitude.
  • Go back in timeA date slider moves back up to 30 days. NASA publishes these maps as daily frames, so it is days, not hours. Forecasts are not part of the data.
Design

Calm and accessible

  • Built for the keyboardEverything works without a mouse, with visible focus on every control.
  • Quiet by designA calm dark look and a minimal layout. The 3D scenes are decoration and navigation aids, never required: everything works with sound, keyboard and screen reader alone. Motion eases off if your system asks for reduced motion.
  • Real NASA data, liveMaps are fetched from NASA GIBS when you open the page, not recorded in advance.
  • Works on a phoneThe layout adapts to narrow screens, and every control is a large, tappable button. Moving, switching layers and sounds, the spoken summary, mute and the date slider all work by touch.
